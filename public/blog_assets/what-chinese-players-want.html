What Do Chinese Players Want? Adapting Your Game for the Chinese Market

Entering the Chinese gaming market is a tantalising opportunity for indie developers and established publishers alike. China boasts the world's largest gaming audience – over 668 million gamers, nearly half the population. With annual game revenues projected in the hundreds of billions, the Chinese gaming market shows no signs of slowing down. However, succeeding in China requires more than a translated build. Chinese gamer preferences, cultural norms, and regulations differ markedly from the West. To publish games in China successfully, you must adapt your game's content and strategy to local tastes without compromising your creative vision. In this post, we'll explore what Chinese players want – from popular genres and monetisation trends to cultural themes and social features – and provide actionable advice on localising and tailoring your game. We'll also address challenges like regulations (ISBN licenses, censorship) and local platforms (Steam China, WeGame, TapTap), and explain why partnering with a specialist like EightSix Games can be your ideal China game publishing partner for co-publishing, compliance, and marketing.

Understanding Chinese Gamer Preferences

To win Chinese players' hearts, it's crucial to understand their gaming preferences and habits. Here are key factors that drive engagement in China:

  • Popular Genres & Platforms: China is a mobile-first gaming market, with over 60% of gamers playing on smartphones. Massively multiplayer RPGs, MOBAs, and strategy titles dominate the charts, reflecting a taste for competitive and cooperative play. For example, Tencent's Honor of Kings (a 5v5 MOBA) has 146 million+ players and is one of China's top titles. MMORPGs and action RPGs are also huge, as are battle royale shooters like PUBG Mobile's Chinese edition. Interestingly, Western casual and puzzle games have found a niche too – titles like Plants vs Zombies 2 and Homescapes became surprise hits, showing that multiple genres can succeed if executed well. On PC, League of Legends remains massively popular with 70+ million players in China. The takeaway: ensure your genre and platform strategy align with Chinese trends (e.g. consider mobile releases or mobile-friendly design if possible).
  • Cultural Themes Matter: Games that resonate with Chinese culture tend to attract passionate followings. Many top domestic games draw on Chinese history and mythology – for instance, NetEase's Fantasy Westward Journey is based on the classic novel Journey to the West and has over 200 million players. Chinese gamers appreciate familiar themes, folklore heroes, and aesthetics. Western titles can tap into this by incorporating cultural elements or events. Creative Assembly's strategy game Total War: Three Kingdoms (set in ancient China) became the franchise's fastest-selling title largely by capturing Chinese players' imagination with a beloved historical setting. Even without fully changing your game's story, you can localise content to include Chinese festival events, mythology-inspired skins, or references that make locals feel seen. At the same time, avoid cultural faux pas – for example, be mindful of symbols or story points that might not translate well. A bit of cultural adaptation goes a long way toward making players feel at home in your game.
  • Monetisation Trends (Gacha, IAPs, Battle Pass): Free-to-play monetisation is king in China. Chinese gamers are generally comfortable spending on in-game purchases – in fact, they spend up to 50% more on IAPs than players elsewhere. Popular monetisation mechanics include gacha (random loot box draws for characters or items), a staple in top RPGs like Genshin Impact, and "pay-to-win" purchases in competitive games. Battle passes are also extremely prevalent – over 25% of top-grossing mobile games in China use a battle pass or subscription system. Successful Western games often integrate these models when entering China, if they hadn't already. The key is to align your monetisation with local expectations: Chinese players are accustomed to ongoing microtransactions, seasonal passes with exclusive rewards, and frequent special offers. Make sure to integrate local payment methods too – Alipay, WeChat Pay, and UnionPay are standard for Chinese users, whereas credit cards might be less common.
  • Social and Community Features: Gaming in China is a highly social activity. Many Chinese players connect through games to socialise with friends or make new ones. Popular titles often encourage teamwork, guilds, and communication. For example, Honor of Kings' success is partly due to its seamless integration with WeChat and QQ – players can log in with their social accounts and easily invite friends or guild members to play. Features like in-game chat, friend lists, clans/guilds, and ranked leaderboards are not just nice-to-have – they're expected. Chinese gamers also value dev interaction and community events. Building an active community on local platforms (WeChat groups, Weibo discussions, Bilibili streams) can significantly boost engagement. Ensure your game supports social play (co-op modes, PvP, team matchmaking) and think about community engagement as part of your live operations. A strong social layer will help your game spread via word-of-mouth in China's interconnected gamer networks.

Adapting Your Game for China (Without Losing Your Vision)

Adapting a game to meet Chinese tastes and rules doesn't mean abandoning your core creative vision. It's about localising smartly and adding flexibility so your game appeals to Chinese players while staying true to itself. Here's how you can localise and optimise your game for China:

  • Deep Localisation & Culturalisation: Proper localisation goes beyond translating text. Hire experienced Chinese translators who can capture your game's tone and adjust idioms or humour to land with the local audience. Also consider cultural localisation – modifying or supplementing content to make it relatable. This could mean referencing Chinese folklore or pop culture where appropriate, or adding China-exclusive content (e.g. special skins, characters, or holiday events for Lunar New Year). Western games often contain references or legendary figures that Chinese players may not recognise; swap in analogous Chinese legends or historical heroes to create a connection. Crucially, ensure your Chinese text fits nicely in UI (Chinese script is more dense – use clear fonts and adequate sizes). Pay attention to colour symbolism and design preferences too. Studies note that Chinese gamers tend to prefer more colourful, content-rich interfaces than Western minimalism. Don't be afraid to make your menus and HUD pop a bit more to suit local tastes.
  • UI/UX Tweaks: Adapting the user interface and experience can significantly improve acceptance. For example, Chinese written characters take up different space than Latin letters – you may need to redesign certain menus or captions so text isn't cramped. Ensure support for Simplified Chinese (the standard writing system in mainland China) and test all dialogues and labels after translation. Additionally, Chinese players are used to certain layouts and features – for instance, scrolling bullet chats ("danmu") overlaying videos, or busy home screens with lots of events and offers in mobile games. While you shouldn't clutter your UI unnecessarily, be mindful that an interface that is too sparse might feel empty to Chinese users. Incorporating familiar UI patterns from popular Chinese games (like clear icons for store, chat, welfare/bonus sections, etc.) can make new players feel at home. It's also wise to implement seamless social logins (WeChat/QQ) and sharing options, as well as accommodate local networks' quirks. Little touches in UX – from using lucky colours (red/gold) for positive feedback to avoiding culturally unlucky numbers – show players that the game is designed with them in mind.
  • Gameplay & Monetisation Balance: You shouldn't redesign your core gameplay for China, but subtle adjustments can help. Chinese players often enjoy a fast progression loop with lots of achievements and rewards – they lean towards "Completion and Competition" over pure exploration. So, consider adding extra achievement systems, daily login bonuses, or competitive ladders if your game lacks them. Monetisation may need adaptation too. If your game is premium (paid up front) in the West, note that China is overwhelmingly free-to-play – it might be necessary to adopt an F2P model or a hybrid for the Chinese release to attract players. If your game already F2P, ensure your IAP store, gacha draws, or battle pass offerings are localised (pricing in RMB, culturally relevant item designs, adherence to any disclosure laws on loot box odds, etc.). Chinese gamers love content, so generous starter rewards and frequent sales or limited-time items can boost goodwill. Remember that Chinese players are enthusiastic spenders when engaged – PUBG Mobile's Chinese version (Peacekeeper Elite) garners 50 million daily active users and China makes up 66% of global player spending on PUBG Mobile. The opportunity is huge if you get the monetisation right. Just be careful to avoid paywalling essential gameplay too heavily – striking a fair balance is key to retaining players long-term.
  • Live Operations & Updates: In China's fast-paced market, games live or die by their live operations. Chinese gamers expect a steady stream of new content, events, and improvements. Unlike Western markets, where a game might go many months between major updates, Chinese players favor titles that . Plan a content cadence that delivers regular updates – whether new levels, characters, seasonal events, or gameplay modes – to keep your community engaged. Live ops also means running in-game events tailored to Chinese holidays (e.g. Chinese New Year, Golden Week) and participating in local gaming festivals or e-sports seasons. Additionally, be prepared for 24/7 live service in terms of community management and customer support. China's players are vocal and value responsive support; having a local team or partner to handle community feedback (in Chinese) on platforms like Weibo, Tieba forums, or WeChat is crucial. By treating your game as a living service – with culturally relevant updates and active community engagement – you'll build loyalty and longevity in the Chinese market.

Navigating Chinese Regulations and Platforms

No discussion of the Chinese market is complete without addressing the regulatory environment and unique distribution landscape. China has strict regulations on game content and a requirement that games be licensed for official release. Here's what you need to know:

  • Regulatory Hurdles (ISBN License & Censorship): To legally publish games in China, you must obtain an ISBN license from the National Press and Publication Administration (NPPA). This is a government-issued approval that involves a thorough content review and can take 8–12+ months for foreign titles. Only a limited number of foreign games get approved each year (just ~110 imports were approved in the past year), so competition is fierce. Working with a local publishing partner is essentially mandatory – foreign developers cannot directly apply for ISBN and must have a Chinese publisher sponsor the game. During the approval process, be prepared to make content changes to satisfy Chinese censorship and regulations. Common requirements include removing or altering anything that violates cultural or political guidelines. For example, games often must remove blood or change its colour, eliminate skeletons or skull imagery, and cut any references to sensitive topics like politics, religion, or territorial disputes. One famous case is World of Warcraft's China release: all skeletons in the game were "fleshed out" or replaced with graves to comply with regulations. The regulators also enforce rules to protect youth (such as limiting playtime for minors and requiring real-ID login systems). Staying compliant is non-negotiable – failure can mean your game is denied a license or even pulled from stores post-launch. Plan for a lengthy review and build flexibility for content changes. It's wise to design your game so that disallowed content (gore, certain symbols) can be toggled off or swapped in the Chinese version without too much hassle.
  • Distribution Platforms – Steam China, WeGame, TapTap (and Steam Global): China's platform ecosystem differs from the West. Console gaming is small due to past bans (though growing now), and PC and mobile dominate. On PC, the major official channels are Steam China (a curated, legally compliant version of Steam run by Valve and Perfect World) and Tencent WeGame (Tencent's own distribution platform). Both require your game to have an ISBN license to be listed. However, many Chinese PC gamers still access the international Steam Global client (which, as of now, remains accessible and has become hugely popular – Simplified Chinese recently surpassed English as the most common language on Steam!). This presents an interesting workaround: some foreign developers launch on Steam global (which doesn't require Chinese approval) to quietly reach Chinese players. This approach carries some risk – the regulatory grey area could change – but it has allowed games like Elden Ring and numerous indies to gain massive Chinese playerbases even without official approval. If you go this route, you'll still want to localise the game and perhaps partner with a Chinese co-publisher to handle marketing, community, and payment support while avoiding the full ISBN process. For mobile games, distribution is fragmented among many Android app stores (run by Tencent, Huawei, Oppo, etc.) since Google Play doesn't operate in China, plus the iOS App Store which does operate but under China's rules. To publish on these official mobile stores, ISBN approval is again required. A popular alternative for mobile (especially for indie or pre-release titles) is TapTap – a third-party app store and community hub where many gamers discover new titles. TapTap often features foreign games (and user reviews) even if they're not officially licensed yet, making it a valuable platform to build hype and get user feedback. In recent years, even Mobile Legends: Bang Bang (a globally popular MOBA) re-entered China via TapTap and a beta test after securing NPPA approval. Summary: For PC, decide between seeking an official launch (Steam China/WeGame with a license) or leveraging Steam global's reach with a local partner. For mobile, plan to either obtain a license for the main app stores or use platforms like TapTap to soft-launch and community-build while you navigate approval. Each path has pros and cons, but all require understanding China's unique platform environment.
  • Local Social Media and Marketing Channels: In China, typical Western marketing channels (Google Play store, Facebook, Twitter) are unavailable or ineffective. Instead, games are promoted via Chinese social media, forums, and streaming platforms. Key channels include WeChat (ubiquitous messaging app with gaming mini-programs), Weibo (microblogging, akin to Twitter), Douyin (the Chinese TikTok, huge for game ads and virality), Bilibili (video streaming popular with gamers, for trailers and developer vlogs), and specialized game communities (Tieba forums, Zhihu Q&A, etc.). Partnering with local KOLs (influencers/streamers) can massively raise your game's profile. Also consider the "Big Four" tech giants in China's internet: Baidu, Tencent, Alibaba, ByteDance – each offers advertising and discovery channels for games (from Baidu SEO to Tencent's QQ and video platforms). Adapting your marketing strategy to this ecosystem is as important as adapting the game itself. Community events, esports tournaments, and fan engagement (art contests, etc.) are also powerful, given how community-driven Chinese gaming is. Overall, be ready to handle a very different digital landscape for promotion and player engagement.

Examples of Western Games Succeeding in China

Many Western-developed games have navigated these challenges and found success in China. Here are a few examples and lessons from their journeys:

  • Clash of Clans – Supercell's world-famous strategy mobile game became a hit in China after partnering with Tencent for local publishing. Tencent released a localised version of CoC with exclusive skins, scenery and ranked features tailored to Chinese users. The result: China quickly became one of CoC's top markets, with high daily engagement. Lesson: team up with local giants and offer China-specific content to delight players.
  • League of Legends – Originally from Riot Games (USA), LoL's rise in China is legendary. After Tencent acquired Riot, LoL was aggressively promoted in China, fully localised in language and voice-overs, and integrated into the burgeoning esports scene. China now hosts the largest LoL player base (70+ million active players) and huge annual revenues. LoL's success shows the importance of culturalisation and community – Riot added Chinese-inspired skins/champions over time and supported local tournaments (LPL) that turned top players into stars. Lesson: invest in long-term community building and adapt content to cultural moments (e.g., special Lunar New Year in-game events).
  • PUBG / Game for Peace (Peacekeeper Elite) – The battle royale PlayerUnknown's Battlegrounds was a global sensation, but to officially launch in China, PUBG's developers had to work with Tencent to create a modified version. The Chinese release, titled Peacekeeper Elite, removed blood and made other cosmetic changes to meet strict content standards (for example, defeated players wave goodbye instead of dying). These changes paid off hugely: Peacekeeper Elite now boasts 50 million daily active users in China and China constitutes the majority of PUBG Mobile's global revenue. Lesson: censorship compliance and localisation can unlock massive user bases – adapt where needed, and your game can thrive under a new identity in China's market.
  • World of Warcraft – Blizzard's MMO has been in China since 2005 via local operators (The9 and later NetEase). WoW had to undergo heavy content changes (no skeletons, toned-down gore) to appease regulators, and releases lagged behind the West due to approval delays. Despite this, WoW built a loyal Chinese fanbase in the millions, showing that core gameplay can shine through if cultural and regulatory barriers are managed. Blizzard's partnership with NetEase provided essential infrastructure: local servers, customer support, and community management (including dealing with a unique ID system to enforce playtime limits on youths). Lesson: stability and long-term partnership are key – be patient and persistent, and work with a trusted local partner to handle evolving regulations and operations over a game's lifespan.

Each of these cases illustrates that while the Chinese market demands adaptation, Western games can and do succeed in China. By learning from their strategies – localisation with cultural flair, partnering with Chinese publishers, adjusting monetisation and complying with content rules – you can chart a path for your own game's entry into China.
